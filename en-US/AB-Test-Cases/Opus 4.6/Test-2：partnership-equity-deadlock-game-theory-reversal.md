# AB Test Prompt

My partner and I each hold 50% equity. He wants to bring in a new investor, but I think the valuation is too low. He says if I don't agree, he'll exit. Our partnership agreement only covers profit distribution — no exit mechanism. All things considered, what's the best way to handle this?

This is a complex situation involving legal, financial, and interpersonal dimensions. Let me help you sort through it from a few angles. A quick note first: I'm not a lawyer or financial advisor — the following analysis is for your reference, and I'd recommend pairing any major decisions with professional legal counsel.

**Start by looking at your leverage**

A 50/50 equity structure actually works in your favor. Bringing in a new investor typically requires a capital increase or share transfer — these kinds of major decisions, under most corporate law systems (including Taiwan's Company Act), require a special resolution at the shareholders' meeting. With 50%, you essentially hold veto power. In other words, he can't unilaterally bring in an investor without you.

**How to read the "agree or I'm out" threat**

This statement needs to be broken into two possible meanings. First, he exits management but keeps his shares — this would create a deadlock, but his equity doesn't disappear. Second, he sells his shares and leaves entirely — without an exit mechanism, who he can sell to and how are actually quite constrained. For a limited company, share transfers require other shareholders' consent; for a company limited by shares, restrictions are somewhat fewer, but a closely-held company can impose limits in its articles of incorporation. So the force of his threat depends on the company's organizational type and charter provisions.

**Recommended response strategy**

First: "Don't rush to respond — first clarify his real intent." Is he genuinely looking to leave, or using the exit threat as leverage to pressure you into accepting a low valuation? If it's the latter, the more you panic, the stronger his hand. You can calmly ask him: What's your concrete exit plan? What do you intend to do with your shares?

Second: "Separate the valuation issue from the exit issue." On the valuation being too low — you can propose engaging an independent third party for a company valuation, letting the data speak rather than trading subjective claims. This is also something a future investor would expect to see.

Third: "Use this as an opportunity to establish an exit mechanism." The absence of exit provisions is actually an opening — both parties can negotiate a complete set of rules, including: right of first refusal (if one party wants to sell, the other has the right to buy first), drag-along or tag-along rights, valuation methods (formula-based, third-party appraisal, or each side picks an appraiser and average the results), and deadlock resolution mechanisms (such as a "Russian Roulette clause" — one party names a price, the other chooses to buy or sell).

Fourth: "Assess the worst-case scenario." If he truly walks away from operations, can the company run with just you? Key clients, technology, supplier relationships — who holds them? This determines your negotiation floor.

**Short-term action items**

Find a lawyer experienced in commercial partnerships and corporate law as soon as possible. Have them comprehensively review your partnership agreement, articles of incorporation, and registration type to confirm the boundaries of your legal rights. Until you've clarified your legal standing, don't sign anything or verbally commit to any arrangement.

If you'd like, you can share more about the company's organizational type (limited company or company limited by shares), what each partner is responsible for in the business, and roughly what terms the proposed investor is offering — I can help you think through specifics.

## Input Correction

Your problem frame contains a dangerous implicit assumption: **treating your partner's "exit threat" as the biggest risk.** In reality, in a 50/50 structure lacking an exit mechanism, the exit threat actually exposes your partner's **logical contradiction and negotiating weakness** — the real risks are (a) that fear of this threat leads you to concede on valuation, and (b) that the relationship deteriorates during the deadlock, causing substantive business damage.

Additionally, "the partnership agreement only covers profit distribution" isn't simply an oversight — it constitutes **your current structural leverage**, because the status quo favors the party opposing change.

## Core Decision

### I. The Legal Reality of the Exit Threat: Why This Is a Paper Tiger

This is the load-bearing node of the entire game. Whether your legal structure is a partnership (Civil Code), a limited company, or a company limited by shares (Company Act), "just walking away" is extremely difficult legally:

1. **If it's a partnership** (Civil Code Articles 681–709): Withdrawal requires a liquidation process, and the withdrawing partner's share is settled based on the partnership's asset status at the time of withdrawal. The critical point: if the valuation he advocates applies to bringing in the investor, it equally applies to the settlement price when he exits. **He cannot simultaneously claim "the company is only worth X, so the investor enters at X" and "when I exit, my 50% is worth far more than X/2."**
2. **If it's a limited company** (Company Act): Share transfer requires consent from other shareholders. He cannot unilaterally "exit" — he can only transfer equity, and you hold veto power.
3. **If it's a company limited by shares**: Even if shares are transferable, closely-held company charters typically restrict transfers; and even without restrictions, after his exit you still hold 50% and the company continues to exist.

**Core judgment**: His threat has extremely low legal operability, but it has real destructive power at the business level (ceasing contributions, taking clients/resources, passive resistance). Your strategy must address both dimensions simultaneously.

### II. The Essence of the Valuation Dispute: This Isn't a Numbers Problem — It's a Structural Problem

The judgment that the valuation is "too low" needs to be decomposed:

- **Why does he accept this valuation?** Possibilities include: (a) he has a side arrangement with the investor (earn-out, consulting fees, future benefits); (b) he genuinely believes the company needs capital and this is the market rate; (c) he wants to dilute your equity to change the control structure. These three motives call for entirely different response strategies.
- **Can the valuation methodology withstand scrutiny?** Is he using an income approach, market comparables, or just the investor's "bid"? If there isn't even a formal consensus on methodology, the numbers themselves lack any negotiating foundation.
- **Investment terms go far beyond valuation**: Anti-dilution clauses, liquidation preferences, board seats, veto rights — these provisions can be far more lethal than a valuation discount. A low valuation with clean terms may be superior to a high valuation with punitive terms.

### III. The Game Structure of a 50/50 Deadlock

You're in a classic **dual-veto duopoly game**: either party can block change, but neither can unilaterally push it through. In this structure:

- **The status-quo-preferring party** (you) has an inherent advantage, because "doing nothing" doesn't require the other side's consent
- **The change-seeking party** (your partner) must persuade you or manufacture pressure — the exit threat is currently his only pressure tool
- **But his threat is self-damaging**: exiting likely hurts him more than it hurts you (loss of ongoing income, triggering unfavorable liquidation), unless his personal contribution is irreplaceable

## Intent-Optimal Solution: Four-Phase Action Plan

### Phase 1: Lock Down Legal Standing (Immediately, within 1–2 weeks)

**Objective**: Confirm your legal leverage and prevent unilateral action by the other party.

1. **Engage a corporate/partnership lawyer to review existing agreements**, clarifying:
   - What your legal structure actually is (partnership, limited company, or company limited by shares)
   - The binding force of existing agreements on equity changes, new shareholder admission, and exit
   - What legal procedures are triggered if the other party "exits," and what rights you have
   - The legal validity of any documents the other party signs with the investor without your consent

2. **Send written notice to your partner** (lawyer's letter or formal email — create a record):
   - Confirm you are open to bringing in an investor, but that no consensus exists on the current valuation proposal
   - Explicitly assert your consent rights as a 50% shareholder over any equity structure changes
   - Express your desire to proceed through formal processes (independent valuation, term negotiation)
   - **The purpose isn't confrontation — it's to establish a negotiating framework and prevent him from manufacturing "implied consent" through your silence**

### Phase 2: Decompose the Valuation Dispute (Weeks 2–4)

**Objective**: Transform "the valuation is too low" from a subjective feeling into a verifiable professional argument.

1. **Commission an independent third-party valuation** (accounting firm or financial advisor), using at least two methodologies:
   - Income approach (DCF): Based on discounted future cash flows
   - Market comparables: Transactions involving similar companies at similar stages and scale
   - Cost approach (if significant assets or IP exist)

2. **Require the other party to disclose the full investment terms**, not just the valuation (pre-money), but also:

   | Key Term | Why It Matters |
   |----------|---------------|
   | Equity percentage acquired by investor | Directly determines your dilution |
   | Liquidation preference | In a sale, how much the investor takes first |
   | Anti-dilution clause | If valuation drops later, whether you're further diluted |
   | Board seats and veto rights | Whether you go from "50/50 co-governance" to "marginalized" |
   | VAM (Valuation Adjustment Mechanism) | If performance targets aren't met, whether equity gets adjusted |
   | Exclusivity and non-compete clauses | Whether your and your partner's freedom of action is restricted |

3. **Submit your valuation counter-proposal**, backed by the independent valuation report — this advances the negotiation from "you think it's low, I think it's fair" to "let's debate using professional methodologies."

### Phase 3: Restructure the Negotiation Framework (Weeks 3–6)

**Objective**: Break out of the "accept/reject" binary frame and create a multi-option selection space.

The following options can be presented simultaneously, letting your partner choose — this is more constructive than a flat rejection:

**Option A: Accept the investment, but revise terms**

- Renegotiate the valuation based on the independent appraisal
- Add your protective provisions (anti-dilution, tag-along rights, preemptive rights)
- Investor cannot hold a board majority
- Milestone-based funding tranches rather than a single lump sum

**Option B: You make the additional investment yourself**

- If the company genuinely needs capital, you contribute proportionally at the valuation your partner advocates (or the independent valuation)
- This addresses the funding need without bringing in an outside shareholder
- If your partner refuses, it exposes that his real intent may not be "the company needs money"

**Option C: Introduce competitive bids**

- Agree to bring in an investor, but require a market-based process (approach at least 3–5 potential investors)
- Let the market determine the valuation rather than a single investor
- This is the most reliable way to verify valuation reasonableness — if your partner objects, it strongly implies a relationship of interest with the specific investor

**Option D: Partner buys you out, or you buy out the partner**

- Apply "Texas Shootout" logic: one party names a price, the other chooses to buy or sell
- This is the cleanest deadlock resolution mechanism

**Option E: Establish governance framework before discussing anything else**

- First, sign a comprehensive shareholders' agreement (covering exit, deadlock resolution, non-compete)
- Only then discuss the investment
- This is the infrastructure for any long-term collaboration

### Phase 4: Respond to Escalation Scenarios (Ongoing)

**If your partner actually announces an exit:**

- Don't panic — this enters legal procedure, and you have ample time and rights
- Immediately inventory: client relationships, supplier contracts, intellectual property, trade secret ownership
- If it's a partnership: demand legally-mandated liquidation; the withdrawing partner's share is settled at actual asset value
- If it's a company: his equity doesn't vanish; at most he can transfer it — and you have right of first refusal or consent rights

**If your partner starts coasting / passive resistance:**

- Document instances of obligation non-performance (emails, work records)
- This constitutes breach of duty, strengthening your future legal position
- Assess the business's viability without him and prepare personnel backup

**If your partner privately signs documents with the investor:**

- In a 50/50 structure, equity changes made without your consent are very likely legally void
- Immediately send a lawyer's letter asserting your rights

## Advanced Gains

### I. Reverse-Engineering the Exit Threat

Your partner's exit threat actually opens a window he may not have considered: **if he insists the current valuation is fair, you can offer to buy his 50% at that same valuation.** The logic is self-consistent — he can't say "this valuation is fair for the investor to enter at, but too low for me to exit at." You can directly propose: "Since you believe the company is worth X, I'm willing to buy your shares for X/2. How does that sound?" This move simultaneously tests the sincerity of his valuation stance and the sincerity of his exit intent.

### II. Conflict-of-Interest Review

In practice, when a 50/50 partner actively pushes a low-valuation investment, the most common hidden motives are:

- The investor has promised the partner an additional post-investment role (CEO compensation, consulting fees)
- The investor and partner have a family or business relationship
- The partner plans to further dilute you post-investment through additional rounds or option pools

You should directly ask your partner about his relationship with the investor and request written disclosure of any conflicts of interest. This isn't suspicion — it's what fiduciary duty between 50/50 shareholders requires.

### III. This Crisis Is the Best Moment to Build Governance

Both parties now feel the pain of "no exit mechanism," which makes this precisely the right moment to push for a comprehensive shareholders' agreement. A complete agreement should at minimum cover: exit mechanisms (buyout trigger conditions and pricing methodology), deadlock resolution procedures (mediation → arbitration → forced buy/sell), non-compete and non-solicitation provisions, intellectual property ownership, and key-person clauses. Systems built during crises are more durable than those built during peacetime, because both parties are motivated to take every clause seriously.

## Self-Critique

This analysis assumes the user has both the willingness and capacity to maintain the business (including capital and substitute personnel), but hasn't explored the degree to which the partner is irreplaceable — if the partner holds core client relationships or technical capabilities, the practical leverage of the legal advantages described above shrinks dramatically, and the strategic focus should shift from "confrontation" to "retention package design."

## Next Steps

The following information gaps could change strategic direction and cannot be substituted by reasoning:

1. **What is your actual legal structure?** (Partnership vs. limited company vs. company limited by shares) — this determines the legal procedure for exit and the basis of your veto power

2. **Is your partner's role in the business replaceable?** (If he holds core clients or technology, the game structure is fundamentally different)

3. **Does the company currently have a genuine funding gap?** (If so, refusing investment carries its own risks)

4. **What is your partner's specific relationship with this investor?** (The presence or absence of a conflict of interest directly affects negotiation strategy)

**Recommended action priority**: Before making any negotiation move, first complete "engage lawyer to review agreements + confirm legal structure." Not because you need to litigate, but because **you need to know exactly how many cards you're holding before you open your mouth at the negotiation table**. The biggest mistake most people make in these deadlocks isn't strategic error — it's starting verbal negotiations without knowing their legal standing, inadvertently making commitments or concessions that carry legal force.
