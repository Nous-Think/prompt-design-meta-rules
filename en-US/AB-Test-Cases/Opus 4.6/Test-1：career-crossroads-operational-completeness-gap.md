# AB Test Prompt

I've been at my current job for three years. Not learning anything new anymore, but the colleagues are great and the pay's decent. Recently got an offer with a 15% raise, but I've heard that company works you to the bone.

# Baseline Response (Opus 4.6)

<details>
<summary>Expand full response</summary>

This is a very typical career dilemma. A few angles to help you think it through:

**The weight of "not learning anything"** is actually heavier than it feels right now. Three years without growth looks comfortable on the surface, but your market competitiveness is quietly eroding. You can still get an offer with a 15% raise today — that might not be the case in another two or three years. So this isn't just a question of "should I jump or not," it's also "how much longer can I afford to jump."

**Good colleague relationships are real, but easily overestimated.** After leaving a job, you typically stay in touch with only a handful of former colleagues, and strong relationships don't break just because you're at different companies. On the flip side, you'll meet new people at the new company too.

**"I've heard it's brutal" needs more digging.** This is the area most worth investing effort in before making your decision. A few directions to explore:

What does "brutal" actually mean? Is it long hours but valuable work that builds your skills? Or is it purely chaotic management and pointless overtime? The former might be tough but worthwhile; the latter is just draining. You can try asking more questions during the interview process — things like the team's turnover rate, typical end-of-day times, how project decisions get made. You could also check reviews on job platforms, or reach out through your network to find someone who's worked there.

**A 15% raise isn't a decisive factor on its own.** If the new job lets you learn new things and strengthens your résumé, it'd be worth considering even at the same salary. Conversely, if it's just 15% more but you're ground down physically and mentally, that's not a good trade either.

I'd suggest reframing the question from "should I go or not" to "what kind of person do I want to become in the next two to three years." If your current place can't help you move in that direction, then maybe not this particular offer — but it's definitely time to start moving.

## Input Correction

You've framed this as a binary — "comfort zone vs. more pay but brutal" — but the real decision variables have been compressed in two places:

**"Not learning anything" is being treated as a static description, when it's actually a dynamic risk.** Skills are a depreciating asset. Three years of stagnation means your market value is declining in relative terms. You feel the pay is "decent" right now because you haven't tested your actual market price — and that price gets revised downward with each additional year of stagnation.

**"I've heard it's brutal" is unverified hearsay, yet you're treating it as a confirmed decision weight.** "Brutal" has at least four completely different meanings (high-intensity high-growth, toxic management, fast-paced high-performance, pure exploitation), each leading to entirely different conclusions. Using uncategorized hearsay as decision input is essentially rolling dice.

### Core Decision

The decision axis here isn't "stay or leave" but rather: **can you afford the compounding cost of continued stagnation, and are you capable of converting hearsay into verified intelligence?**

Key assumptions and professional baselines supporting this judgment:

**Career Capital Theory (Cal Newport)**: Skills, network, and credibility constitute your career capital stock. A flat learning curve means you're depleting stock rather than accumulating it. Each additional year increases the chance that the position level available to you when switching will be revised downward.

**The three-year critical point**: In most industries, three years is the implicit "move up or move out" clock. Beyond this point without role expansion or a technical leap, your résumé narrative shifts from "stable" to "stagnant" in the eyes of your next employer.

**Decision weight of colleague relationships**: The relationships are real, but in career decision-making they belong to the category of "maintainable, non-exclusive assets" — leaving doesn't mean severing ties. If this becomes your primary reason for staying, you're effectively trading long-term career trajectory for short-term emotional comfort.

**The real meaning of 15%**: Taken at face value, 15% is a standard job-switching premium — not generous. But the significance of salary isn't in the absolute number; it's in whether it comes with a steeper growth curve. If the new role positions you to command a 30–50% increase at your next move in two years, this 15% is an entry ticket, not a destination. Conversely, if the new company is just extracting your existing skills without adding new capital, 15% is all you're getting in exchange for your health.

### Intent-Optimal Solution

What you actually need isn't a "go or stay" answer, but a **checklist for converting unknowns into knowns before making an irreversible decision**:

**Step 1: Verify the nature of "brutal" (the unknown with the highest decision weight)**

Don't rely on hearsay. Concrete approach:

**Find people who have worked at that company — at least two, ideally one current and one former employee.** Search LinkedIn for the company + your target department, look for mutual connections or send cold messages directly. Former employees are typically far more candid than current ones.

**Ask the right questions.** Don't ask "is it brutal there?" — that only gets emotional answers. Ask structured questions instead:

"Roughly how many hours do you actually work per week? How often do you need to respond to messages on weekends?" (quantifying work hours)

"What was your biggest skill gain there? What capabilities did you take away that you didn't have before?" (verifying growth potential)

"How does your direct manager handle deadline conflicts? What's the team's response pattern when things go wrong?" (distinguishing toxic management vs. high standards)

"What's the approximate turnover rate? What's the main reason people leave?" (systemic signal)

**Observe the interview process itself.** Communication style during interviews, response speed, and the degree to which they dodge your questions are all real-time samples of company culture.

**Step 2: Quantify the cost of stagnation (the risk you're currently ignoring)**

Answer three questions for yourself:

"If I went out and interviewed at five companies right now, what level of position and salary could I get?" — If you're not sure, that uncertainty itself is a warning sign, indicating you've lost touch with the market.

"How does my work content from the past year differ substantively from two years ago?" — If the answer is "pretty much the same," your résumé has had no new material for two years.

"If I stay another two years, will my tech stack / management experience / industry knowledge be worth more than today?" — If the answer is vague or leaning negative, you're paying hidden tuition for comfort.

**Step 3: Expand the compressed option space (this is not a binary choice)**

You have at least four paths, not two:

| Path | Action | When it applies |
|------|--------|-----------------|
| **A. Accept the new offer** | After verifying the nature of "brutal," accept if it's the "high-intensity, high-growth" type | Growth curve clearly steeper than current role |
| **B. Decline but start job hunting** | Reject this offer, but treat it as a signal — you've been here three years, time to seriously survey the market | "Brutal" verified as toxic type, but stagnation problem still needs solving |
| **C. Use the offer to negotiate with current employer** | Leverage the offer to discuss with your current manager: raise, transfer, new projects, expanded responsibilities | You still hypothesize a growth path at current company, and your manager has resources and willingness |
| **D. Stay but set a deadline** | Give yourself an explicit 6 months: if no substantive growth change, then start job hunting | You judge there are untapped internal opportunities, but they require proactive pursuit |

**Operational details for Path C** (most people won't think of this, but it's often the highest expected-value choice):

Don't negotiate with threats ("I've got an offer, I'm leaving"). Use a collaborative frame instead: "I've been approached by the market recently, which made me seriously think about my development here. I value this team a lot, but I need to see clear growth space in the coming year. Can we talk about that?"

What you want isn't just a raise (a raise is just a painkiller) but a **change in responsibilities**: leading new projects, cross-departmental rotation, tech stack upgrades, management opportunities — these are real career capital.

If your manager's response is a vague "let me look into it" without a concrete timeline and specifics, that response itself is your signal to leave.

## Advanced Gains

**Reframing "great colleagues."** Good colleague relationships do have value, but the form of that value needs precise identification: is it "these people make me better" (growth relationship) or "these people make me comfortable" (comfort relationship)? The former is a professional reason to stay; the latter is emotional anchoring. The distinguishing question: if you dropped these colleagues into a more challenging environment, would you all level up together, or would you reminisce about the old days?

**Salary benchmark calibration.** "Pay's decent" is a judgment without an anchor. I'd recommend doing a market calibration: check Glassdoor, Levels.fyi (if you're in tech), or a recruiter to confirm the market median and P75 for your role × experience × industry. If you're currently below the median, "decent" is actually "undervalued, but too comfortable to notice."

## Self-Critique

The entire analysis is built on the assumption that "stagnation is the greatest risk." But if the user is at a life stage that structurally requires stability (family pressures, health issues), the risk-weight ranking could be fundamentally different. This dimension was deprioritized without being explored.

## Next Steps

The following information gaps could change the decision direction and cannot be substituted by reasoning:

**What is your industry and function?** The cost of three years of stagnation varies enormously across industries (in tech, three years is nearly a generation; in traditional manufacturing, three years might just be entering the sweet spot).

**What specifically does the new role involve, and how does it differ from your current position?** This determines whether the 15% is buying "more of the same" or "a new capability curve."

**What are your current life-stage constraints?** Caregiving responsibilities, financial leverage (mortgage, etc.), health considerations — these directly change your risk tolerance threshold.

**Proactive recommendation:** Regardless of your final decision, **start interviewing now.** Not to collect offers, but to calibrate against the market. You've gone three years without testing your market price and competitiveness — that information gap is itself a decision blind spot. Interviewing is the lowest-cost form of intelligence gathering, and with an existing offer as your floor, you'll approach it with far more composure than if you were interviewing "naked."
